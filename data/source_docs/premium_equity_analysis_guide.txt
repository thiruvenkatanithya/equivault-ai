# PREMIUM EQUITY ANALYSIS GUIDE

Original EquiVault learning material | Access level: Premium

## Start with the business

Before interpreting a share price, describe how the company earns revenue, its main costs, customers, competitors, and important dependencies. Identify whether reported growth comes from recurring operations, acquisitions, temporary conditions, or accounting changes.

Compare a company with its own history and with relevant peers. Differences in business models, geography, accounting policies, and financial year-end dates can make peer comparisons misleading.

## Read the financial statements together

- Income statement: Review revenue, operating costs, interest, taxes, and net income over multiple periods.
- Balance sheet: Review assets, liabilities, cash, debt, and shareholders' equity at a reporting date.
- Cash-flow statement: Compare cash generated by operations with reported earnings and examine investing and financing cash flows.
- Notes and disclosures: Check accounting methods, segment definitions, commitments, related-party transactions, and material risks.

One figure rarely tells the full story. For example, revenue growth alongside falling operating cash flow may call for closer examination of working capital, collections, or non-cash revenue.

## Common analytical ratios

- Gross margin = (revenue − cost of goods sold) ÷ revenue.
- Operating margin = operating income ÷ revenue.
- Current ratio = current assets ÷ current liabilities.
- Debt-to-equity = total debt ÷ shareholders' equity. Definitions of debt and equity vary, so document the calculation.
- Earnings per share (EPS) = earnings attributable to common shareholders ÷ weighted-average common shares.
- Price-to-earnings (P/E) = share price ÷ earnings per share. A P/E comparison is not meaningful when earnings are negative and can be distorted by cyclical earnings.

Treat ratios as prompts for investigation, not as standalone buy or sell signals. Explain the calculation, date, units, and assumptions used.

## Valuation as a range of scenarios

A valuation estimate depends on uncertain assumptions about future cash flows, growth, margins, risk, and discount rates. A discounted cash flow model converts projected cash flows into an estimated present value; small changes to long-term assumptions can materially change the result.

Use conservative, base, and optimistic assumptions to understand sensitivity. Label model outputs as estimates rather than observed facts. Compare more than one method where appropriate, and state the limitations of each method.

## Research and source hygiene

1. Prefer company filings and audited statements for reported financial history.
2. Record the document name, reporting period, publication date, units, and page or section.
3. Distinguish reported facts from analyst estimates and your own assumptions.
4. Check for restatements, one-time items, diluted shares, and changes in accounting policy.
5. Keep a record of formulas and assumptions so another reader can reproduce the analysis.
